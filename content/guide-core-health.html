Health insurance basics and limited coverage

Test part D

Health insurance has two main jobs: medical expense coverage pays for care, and disability income coverage replaces lost earnings.

CoverageWhat it pays
Medical expenseHospital, surgical, doctor and other costs of care
Disability incomeA monthly benefit while the insured can't work
Accidental death and dismemberment (AD&D)Principal sum for accidental death (and often loss of two limbs or both eyes); capital sum, a smaller set amount, for losing one limb or one eye
Hospital indemnityA fixed amount per day or per stay, whatever the bill
Specified (dread) diseaseOnly the diseases named in the policy, such as cancer
Workers' compensationWork-related injury and illness; required by state law and paid for by employers
  • Accidental means requires both the cause and the result to be accidental. Accidental bodily injury (results) requires only an unexpected result, so it covers more.
  • Nonoccupational coverage excludes job-related losses because workers' compensation covers them. Occupational coverage pays on and off the job.
  • Common exclusions: intentionally self-inflicted injury, war, cosmetic surgery and losses covered by workers' compensation.

Riders

  • Cost of living adjustment (COLA): raises benefits during a long claim to keep up with inflation.
  • Guaranteed insurability option: buy more coverage later as income rises, without proving insurability.
  • Social insurance supplement: pays when Social Security or another program pays less than expected, or nothing.
  • Waiver of premium: stops premiums during a long total disability.

Business uses

  • Business overhead expense: pays rent, utilities and staff salaries while the owner is disabled. It doesn't replace the owner's income.
  • Disability buy-sell: funds the purchase of a disabled owner's share, often as a lump sum after a long elimination period of 1 to 2 years.
  • Key person disability: the business owns the policy, pays for it and receives the benefits.

Medical expense insurance, managed care and the ACA

Test part D

Basic and major medical

  • Basic medical expense covers hospital, surgical and physician costs, often first-dollar but with low limits. Surgical benefits often follow a relative value scale or surgical schedule.
  • Major medical covers large expenses after a deductible, with coinsurance (such as 80/20). The stop-loss caps the insured's out-of-pocket costs; after that the plan pays 100%.
  • Supplemental major medical sits on top of a basic plan, with a corridor deductible between the two. Comprehensive major medical combines both in one policy with one deductible.
  • Common accident: one deductible when several family members are hurt in the same accident. Carryover: expenses in the last three months of a year count toward the next year's deductible.
  • Cost controls include preadmission certification and second surgical opinions.

Managed care

PlanKey features
HMOPrepaid care in a service area; a primary care gatekeeper approves referrals; doctors often paid by capitation; emphasis on preventive care. A staff model (closed panel) employs its doctors; an IPA (open panel) contracts with independent doctors
PPODiscounted network; out-of-network care is covered at a higher cost to the insured

Affordable Care Act and tax-favored accounts

  • Children can stay on a parent's plan until they turn 26, even if they marry or live elsewhere (HealthCare.gov).
  • Individual plans can't refuse coverage or charge more for preexisting conditions.
  • Metal levels show the plan's estimated share of costs: Bronze 60%, Silver 70%, Gold 80%, Platinum 90%. Catastrophic plans are for people under 30 or with a hardship or affordability exemption (HealthCare.gov).
  • HSA: requires a high deductible health plan. Contributions are deductible, qualified withdrawals are tax-free, the balance rolls over, and non-medical withdrawals before 65 owe a 20% additional tax (IRS Pub. 969).
  • HRA: funded only by the employer. FSA: mostly use-it-or-lose-it.

Health policy provisions and renewability

Test part E

Individual health policies use the uniform provisions: 12 mandatory and a set of optional ones. Each state adopts its own version, so check your state's section for its exact numbers.

Mandatory provisionStandard rule
Entire contractThe policy and attached papers are the whole agreement
Time limit on certain defensesMisstatements (except fraudulent ones) can't void the policy after 2 years in most states (3 in some)
Grace period7 days weekly, 10 days monthly, 31 days otherwise
ReinstatementAutomatic if the insurer doesn't act on the application within 45 days; accidents covered right away, sickness after 10 days
Notice of claimWithin 20 days of the loss
Claim formsInsurer sends them within 15 days
Proof of lossWithin 90 days in the standard provision (some states allow longer); no later than 1 year unless the insured lacked legal capacity
Time of payment of claimsImmediately on proof; disability benefits at least monthly
Payment of claimsTo the insured, or the beneficiary for death benefits
Physical exam and autopsyInsurer may examine the insured during a claim, at its own expense
Legal actionsNot before 60 days after proof of loss, and not after 3 years
Change of beneficiaryThe insured can change it unless the designation is irrevocable

Optional provisions include change of occupation (benefits adjust to the new job's risk), misstatement of age, other insurance with this or other insurers, relation of earnings to insurance (benefits limited to earnings), unpaid premium (deducted from a claim), cancellation, conformity with state statutes, illegal occupation, and intoxicants and narcotics.

Renewability, from most to least protective

  1. Noncancelable: can't be canceled, and the premium can't rise, until a stated age.
  2. Guaranteed renewable: must be renewed until a stated age; premiums can rise only for a whole class.
  3. Conditionally renewable: can be nonrenewed only for reasons stated in the policy, never for health alone.
  4. Optionally renewable: the insurer can decline to renew on any anniversary or premium due date.
  5. Cancelable: the insurer can cancel at any time with notice.

Group health and COBRA

Test part E
  • Group coverage is underwritten for the group as a whole. The employer holds the master policy; each member gets a certificate.
  • Blanket policies cover a changing group, such as students or passengers, without naming them. A MEWA lets several small employers band together for benefits.
  • Coordination of benefits: the plan covering a person as an employee pays first. For children covered by both parents, the birthday rule makes the plan of the parent whose birthday comes first in the year primary.
COBRA ruleFederal requirement

PremiumUp to 102% of cost (150% during the disability extension)

Medicare, Medigap and Medicaid

Test part E
PartCovers
A (hospital)Inpatient hospital, skilled nursing facility, hospice and home health care
B (medical)Doctors, outpatient care, durable medical equipment and many preventive services
C (Medicare Advantage)Private plans that bundle A, B and usually D
D (drugs)Prescription drugs

Source: Medicare.gov.

  • Medicare covers people 65 and older, and people under 65 after 24 months of Social Security disability benefits or with end-stage renal disease.
  • Medigap policies fill Medicare's gaps. They're standardized: every company's plan with the same letter has the same benefits.
  • The Medigap open enrollment period is a one-time, 6-month window starting the first month a person is 65 and enrolled in Part B. During it, insurers can't refuse to sell or charge more for health (Medicare.gov).
  • Medicaid is a joint federal and state program for people with limited income and resources.
Watch outMedicare doesn't pay for long-term custodial care. It covers only limited skilled nursing facility care.

Long-term care

Test part E
  • The six activities of daily living: eating, toileting, transferring, bathing, dressing and continence.
  • A tax-qualified policy pays when a licensed health care practitioner certifies, within the past 12 months, that the person can't do at least 2 ADLs for 90 days, or needs substantial supervision because of severe cognitive impairment.
  • A qualified long-term care contract is treated as accident and health insurance for taxes (26 U.S.C. § 7702B).
  • Levels of care run from skilled and intermediate care to custodial care (help with daily activities), plus home health care, adult day care and respite care.
  • Inflation protection raises the benefit amount over time.
