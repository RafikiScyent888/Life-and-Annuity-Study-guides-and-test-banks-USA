Reinsurance

Reinsurance is insurance for insurance companies. It protects the insurer from catastrophic loss.

  • The ceding (original) insurer stays responsible to the client.
  • Treaty reinsurance is broad and automatic.
  • Facultative reinsurance is case by case, for specific risks.

Risk, loss and hazards

Test part A

Insurers cover only pure risk: a chance of loss with no chance of gain. Speculative risk, like buying stock, can lose or gain and is not insurable. A peril causes a loss; a hazard makes a loss more likely.

HazardWhat drives itExample
PhysicalA tangible condition you can see or measureIcy steps, faulty wiring, high blood pressure
MoralDishonesty. IntentionalArson, faking a claim
MoraleCarelessness because “I'm insured.” UnintentionalLeaving the car unlocked
Watch outCareless or bad habits are morale hazard. Moral hazard always means intentional dishonesty.
  • Loss: an unintended reduction in financial value. A direct loss comes from the peril with no intervening cause. An indirect loss is a consequence of a direct loss, such as lost rent after a fire.
  • Accident: sudden and unexpected, at a specific time and place. Occurrence: any event that causes a loss, including gradual exposure. Every accident is an occurrence; not every occurrence is an accident.
  • Accidental means vs accidental results: “means” requires the cause to be accidental. “Results” (accidental bodily injury) only requires the outcome to be unexpected, so it covers more.
  • Lines of coverage: accident and health (illness, injury, disability), life (death), annuities (living too long), property (fire, wind, hail, theft, vandalism), liability (negligence, errors and omissions, third-party claims).
  • Named perils covers only the perils listed. Open (special) perils covers everything not specifically excluded.

Risk management methods: STARR

MethodMeaningExample
SharingSpread risk among several partiesReciprocal exchange, risk pooling
TransferShift risk by contractBuying insurance, hold-harmless clause, reinsurance
AvoidanceEliminate the activity or hazardNot doing the risky activity
ReductionLower the chance or size of lossA company safety program
RetentionDeliberately keep the riskDeductibles, self-insurance
  • Law of large numbers: the larger the group of similar, independent exposure units, the more predictable losses become.
  • Exposure units must be homogeneous: similar objects exposed to the same perils.
  • Adverse selection: higher-risk people seek coverage more, so claims run higher than expected. Underwriting prevents it. Concealing material facts on an application is a form of it.
  • Indemnity: restore the insured to their pre-loss financial position, with no profit. Exceptions: life insurance, valued policies, replacement cost coverage.
Watch out“As the number of insureds increases, the number of losses decreases” is a classic false statement. More insureds make losses more predictable, not fewer.

Insurance contracts, agency and negligence

Test part A

A legal contract needs four elements, CLOC: Competent parties, Legal purpose, Offer and acceptance (agreement), Consideration. The applicant's consideration is the premium and the application statements; the insurer's is the promise to pay. STOLI (stranger-originated life insurance) fails legal purpose because the buyer has no insurable interest.

Contract featureWhat it means
AdhesionThe insurer writes it; the insured takes it or leaves it. Unclear wording is read in the insured's favor
AleatoryUnequal exchange: one party may get far more than it paid in
UnilateralOnly the insurer makes an enforceable promise. “Bilateral” is not an insurance term
ConditionalConditions, like paying the premium and filing a claim, must be met before claims are paid
  • Representations are statements the applicant believes true. A material misrepresentation can void the policy. Warranties are statements guaranteed true.
  • Materiality: the insurer would not have issued the policy had it known the concealed fact. Rescission: the contract is treated as void.
  • Estoppel: the insurer can't deny a right it led the insured to rely on. It applies to a policy already in force. Parol evidence rule: oral statements made before the written contract can't change it.
  • Insurable interest: you would lose money if the insured died. For life insurance it must exist at application only, and it's presumed for yourself, a spouse, close family and business partners.

Life insurance policies

Test part B

Term life is pure, temporary protection for a set period. It has the lowest premium per dollar of coverage and focuses on the death benefit.

Term typeKey feature
Level termFace amount stays the same; lowest premium for a limited time
Decreasing termFace amount drops on a set schedule (mortgages, debts)
Credit lifeDecreasing term that pays off a debt
RenewableRenew without evidence of insurability; the new premium is always higher (attained age)
ConvertibleChange to permanent coverage without evidence of insurability

Converting at attained age uses your age at conversion, so the premium is higher. Original age uses your age when the term policy was issued.

Permanent life

PolicyKey feature
Whole life (straight / ordinary)Permanent, level premium, cash value grows tax-deferred, pays no matter how or when the insured dies
Limited-pay life (e.g. 20-pay)Lifetime coverage, but premiums stop early. Builds cash value faster than straight life. Coverage normally runs to age 100
Single premium whole lifeOne payment; immediate cash value; usually a MEC
Indeterminate premiumStates a maximum guaranteed premium but charges a lower current premium that the insurer can change
Adjustable lifeOwner can change the face amount, premium and cash value
Universal lifeTerm coverage plus a cash value account; flexible premiums
Indexed universal lifeFixed (non-variable) product with interest tied to a market index
Variable lifeCash value and death benefit rise and fall with separate-account investments; the owner bears the investment risk
Joint and survivorInsures two people; pays at the second (last) death
Industrial lifeSmall face amounts with premiums often paid weekly or monthly; some states cap the face amount (see your state's section)
  • Level premium concept: premiums in the early years are higher than the cost of protection. The excess builds the reserve that keeps later premiums level.
  • MEC (modified endowment contract): a policy funded too fast to pass the IRS 7-pay test, such as single premium. Loans and withdrawals become taxable; the death benefit stays tax-free.
  • Corridor: in universal life, the amount of pure insurance above the cash value.
  • Excess interest crediting: the index-linked method ties it to an economic index; the portfolio method ties it to the insurer's own investment earnings.
  • IOLI / STOLI: investors own a policy on someone unrelated to them, so there is no insurable interest.
Watch outRaising the face amount with policy dividends (paid-up additions) is not adjustable life.

How the premium is built

  • Three factors: mortality, interest and expense. Morbidity is not a life premium factor.
  • Net single premium = mortality cost − interest.
  • Gross premium = net premium + loading (the expense factor).

More policy types

  • Endowment: pays the face amount at death during the term, or at the maturity date if the insured is alive.
  • Modified premium: lower premium for the first few years, then one step up to a higher level premium. Graded premium rises gradually each year before leveling off.
  • Jumping juvenile: the face amount increases automatically at a set age, often fivefold at 21, with no premium increase or proof of insurability.
  • Universal life is unbundled: mortality charges, expenses and interest credits are shown separately. The target premium is the amount the insurer suggests paying. Option A pays a level death benefit (the face amount includes the cash value); Option B pays the face amount plus the cash value.
  • Variable products are securities, so selling them also requires FINRA registration, such as the Series 6.

Nonforfeiture options

These guarantee value even if the policy lapses.

  1. Cash surrender: take the cash value and end the policy.
  2. Reduced paid-up: a smaller face amount for life, with no more premiums.
  3. Extended term: the full face amount as term coverage for as long as the cash value lasts.

Dividend options: CRAPO

Cash, Reduction of premium, Accumulate at interest, Paid-up additions, One-year term.

Taxes

A lump-sum death benefit is free of income tax, but interest earned on proceeds held by the insurer is taxable. Cash value grows tax-deferred. Surrender value is the cash paid when the owner voluntarily ends the policy.

Beneficiary designations

  • Per stirpes: a deceased beneficiary's share passes to their descendants. Per capita: it's split among the surviving named beneficiaries.
  • Class designation: a group such as “my children,” which includes children born later.
  • Estate: proceeds go through probate and can be reached by creditors.
  • Minors: name a trust, guardian or custodian, since a minor can't give the insurer a valid release.
  • Irrevocable: the beneficiary must consent before the owner changes them, takes a loan or assigns the policy.
  • Changing a beneficiary: the filing method needs only written notice to the insurer; the endorsement method needs the policy sent in to be endorsed.

Underwriting, the application and delivery

Test part A
  • Underwriting evaluates insurability and assigns a risk class: preferred (better than average), standard (the average baseline), substandard or rated (below average, extra premium), or declined.
  • The field underwriter is the agent, who starts the underwriting process.
  • Sources: the APS (attending physician statement) from the applicant's doctor; the MIB (Medical Information Bureau), which shares medical data among insurers; consumer (credit) reports, which show whether premiums will be paid on time.
  • Exam default: the owner, payor, applicant and insured are the same person unless the question says otherwise. If the insured is not the owner, every party signs. The applicant and the agent always sign.
  • Insurable interest must exist at the time of application for a valid life policy.
  • Discriminating against a protected class is illegal. Charging different rates for real risk differences is allowed.

Receipts High yield

Expect two to four exam questions on receipts.

ReceiptWhen coverage startsWhere it's used
ConditionalThe application date (or medical exam date), but only if the applicant qualifies as applied forMost common in life insurance
BindingImmediately, until the insurer accepts or rejectsSparingly in life; mainly property and casualty

If no premium is paid with the application, there is no coverage until the policy is delivered and the first premium is collected in full.

  • Free look: at least 10 days after delivery in most states to return the policy for a full refund. Some states allow longer, for example for seniors or mail-order sales (see your state's section).
  • Fiduciary responsibility: the producer holds premiums in trust and must act in the client's best interest.
  • Anti-money-laundering red flag: cash value paid out to a third party.
  • Master policy: issued to an employer to cover its employees in a group plan.

Forming the contract

  • The application with the first premium is the applicant's offer. A policy issued as applied for is the insurer's acceptance; a policy issued differently (for example, at a higher premium) is a counteroffer the applicant must accept.
  • If no premium was paid with the application, the agent collects it at delivery along with a statement of continued good health.
  • Corrections on a paper application are crossed out and initialed by the applicant, never erased.
  • Cost comparison: the traditional net cost method (premiums minus dividends and cash value) ignores the time value of money. The interest-adjusted method accounts for it and is used in buyer's guide cost indexes.

Annuities

Test part C

An annuity protects against living too long: it pays out a sum of money through regular payments. It's a contract between the contract owner and the insurer, and only a life insurer can guarantee income for the annuitant's whole life.

PhaseWhat happensWho matters
Accumulation (pay-in)Contributions grow tax-deferred; interest isn't taxed until withdrawnThe contract owner holds the rights; a named beneficiary receives the value if the owner dies
Annuitization (payout)The first payment starts the payout; each payment is part principal, part interestThe annuitant receives income for a stated time or for life
Annuity typeKey feature
SPIA (single premium immediate)One premium; payments begin within 12 months
SPDA (single premium deferred)One premium; payments begin later (at least 1 year)
FPDA (flexible premium deferred)Periodic flexible premiums; payments begin after premiums stop
Equity-indexedFixed (non-variable); interest linked to a stock market index
MVA (market value adjusted)Also called a modified guaranteed annuity
Life annuityPays for the annuitant's life, covering the risk of outliving savings

Qualified plans are funded with pre-tax dollars: IRAs, 403(b) TSAs, 401(k)s and pensions often use annuities. Non-qualified annuities are bought with after-tax dollars.

Payout options

OptionWhat's guaranteed
Straight (pure) lifeHighest income for one person; stops at death, nothing to a beneficiary
Life with period certainPayments for life, and for at least the certain period (e.g. 10 years) even if the annuitant dies sooner
Cash or installment refundAt least the amount paid in is paid out; the rest goes to a beneficiary in cash or installments
Joint and survivorPayments continue until the second of two annuitants dies, sometimes at a reduced amount (for example, two-thirds) after the first death
Joint lifePayments stop at the first death

The longer the guarantee, the smaller each payment: life with a 20-year period certain pays less per month than life with 10 years, and straight life pays the most.

Also on the exam outline

  • The annuity principle: life insurance creates an estate; an annuity liquidates one. Pooled mortality (annuitants who die early fund those who live longer) lets a life annuity pay more than interest alone.
  • Premium methods: single premium, or installments (fixed, flexible or periodic). An immediate annuity can only be bought with a single premium.
  • Who bears the risk: the insurer in a fixed annuity; the owner in a variable annuity. An indexed annuity guarantees a minimum rate and credits extra interest from an index, subject to caps or participation rates.
  • Variable annuity units: accumulation units during the pay-in period, converted at payout to a fixed number of annuity units whose value changes.
  • Guarantee before the annuity starting date: if the owner dies during accumulation, the beneficiary usually gets the greater of the premiums paid or the current value.
  • Surrender charges: a back-end load for cashing in early, usually shrinking each year.
  • Taxes on payments: the exclusion ratio makes part of each payment from a non-qualified annuity a tax-free return of the owner's cost basis.

Taxes

  • Withdrawals before annuitization come out earnings first, taxed as ordinary income. Before age 59½, a 10% penalty applies unless an exception fits, such as death, disability or substantially equal payments (26 U.S.C. § 72).
  • Section 1035 exchanges are tax-free for life to life, life to annuity, and annuity to annuity, but not annuity to life (26 U.S.C. § 1035).

Life insurance needs, business uses and retirement plans

Test part C

How much insurance? The human life value approach puts a dollar value on discounted future earnings and counts only lost income. The needs approach uses charts and tables to add up:

  • Lump-sum needs: final expenses, debt repayment, emergency fund, mortgage payoff, estate conservation, charitable gifts.
  • Income needs: support for survivors, education, retirement supplement, cash and liquidity.

Group life

  • The employer holds the master policy. It must be a natural group, formed for a reason other than buying insurance.
  • Conversion: an employee who leaves can convert to individual whole life within 31 days, with no medical exam or proof of insurability, at attained age, up to the group amount. If they die during the conversion period, the group policy pays.
  • Accidental death is not a type of group life policy.

Retirement plans

PlanKey point
Defined contributionFunded by the employer, the employee or both
403(b) TSATax-sheltered annuity for public school and 501(c)(3) nonprofit employees
SIMPLE IRAFor small employers; simpler, with lower start-up and admin costs

ERISA requirements for qualified plans: a formal written plan, for the sole benefit of employees, meant to be permanent; eligibility at age 21 with 1 year of service; no discrimination in favor of highly paid employees; survivor benefits and minimum vesting; plan assets kept separate from the business.

Vesting: employees are always 100% vested in their own contributions. For employer money in a defined benefit plan: 5-year cliff, or graded from 20% at year 3 to 100% at year 7. Example: Tom is 80% vested, so he forfeits 20% of the employer money.

  • Required minimum distributions start at 73. A missed RMD costs a 25% IRS excise tax.
  • An indirect rollover paid to you from a qualified plan, like profit sharing, has 20% withheld for income tax.
  • IRA rollovers are not limited by dollar amount.

Buy-sell plans and the SEP

  • In a cross-purchase plan the business owners insure each other; in an entity (stock redemption) plan the business owns a policy on each owner.
  • A SEP (Simplified Employee Pension) puts employer contributions into each employee's own IRA with little paperwork.

Watch out“40 credits” is fully insured, not currently insured. Currently insured takes only 6 recent credits.
  • Disability: fully insured plus 20 credits in the 40 quarters before disability; a 5-month waiting period; total disability expected to last 12 months or end in death. Workers under 24 need 6 credits in the 3 years before disability.
  • Survivors: a $255 lump-sum death benefit, plus monthly income based on the worker's PIA. Children receive benefits until 18.

  • Full retirement age: 65 for the oldest workers, rising to 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
  • Benefit period: the time frame for Medicare Part A hospital benefits.
